A Look at Australia’s Broadband Divide

FIT2179 Data Visualisation 2  ·  Monash University  ·  May 2026

Scroll to explore
Australia's National Broadband Network (NBN) promised fast, reliable internet for all Australians — but is everyone actually getting the same experience? This page explores 82,000+ Ookla Speedtest measurements from 2021 Q3 alongside 1,787 monitored household services from the ACCC's March 2025 Measuring Broadband Australia report. Together they reveal where speeds are fast, where they lag behind, which technologies are to blame, and whether Australian households are getting what they pay for.
Part 1

Where is Fast and Where is Slow?

A patchwork of speeds across states

Average fixed broadband download speeds varied noticeably across Australian states in 2021. The ACT led the country at around 61 Mbps, while Western Australia trailed at under 49 Mbps — a gap of over 12 Mbps. The ACT's advantage reflects its higher concentration of FTTP connections and tech-savvy residents who run Speedtest more often on fast connections.

61 Mbps ACT led all states in 2021 Ookla average
Source: Ookla Speedtest Open Data, 2021 Q3 (CC BY 4.0)

Speed follows population density

Zooming in to a 0.5° grid reveals how strongly speed tracks population. Dark blue cells mark the fastest areas, clustering tightly around Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, and Perth. Light orange highlights slower regions — rural coastal zones and the remote interior. Urban tiles often exceed 100 Mbps; remote rural tiles average below 30 Mbps. City labels mark the five largest urban centres where broadband is fastest.

Source: Ookla Speedtest Open Data, 2021 Q3 (CC BY 4.0)
Part 2

Why is it Slow? Technology and Geography

Urban areas outperform rural areas — in every state and across every technology

Whether you look by state (left) or by technology (right), the urban–rural gap is consistent and large. Victoria's urban services averaged over 300 Mbps while Tasmania's minor-rural services managed just 64 Mbps — a 4.6× gap. FTTP delivers blazing speeds in urban areas, while rural communities rely on Fixed Wireless and FTTN, technologies limited by copper wire distance from the node.

Technology share in the monitoring panel

FTTN remains the most common technology in the 2025 panel — a legacy of the early NBN rollout that prioritised copper nodes over full-fibre. However, FTTP is growing rapidly as the government's fibre upgrade programme accelerates nationwide.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025

Fibre pulls far ahead — and is more consistent

The box plot reveals not just average differences but the consistency of each technology. FTTP services cluster tightly at high speeds. FTTN shows a wide spread — some services barely reach 20 Mbps, others hit 100 Mbps, depending on copper line distance. Satellite has the widest spread of all, driven by weather, congestion, and orbital differences between Sky Muster and Starlink.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025

Which providers use which technology?

Not all internet providers offer the same technology mix — their customer bases reflect where they operate and who they target. Aussie Broadband and Leaptel have a high FTTP share, attracting customers who've upgraded to full-fibre. Telstra covers the broadest mix, reflecting its nationwide reach. Starlink is exclusively satellite — the only viable option for many remote Australians with no fixed-line NBN access.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)
Part 3

Are We Getting What We Paid For?

Most services deliver — but not all

Each dot compares a service's advertised plan tier (x-axis) against its actual busy-hour speed (y-axis). Points along the dashed diagonal represent perfect delivery; points below the line are underperforming. FTTP and HFC generally cluster close to the diagonal. FTTN and Fixed Wireless show more scatter, frequently falling short on higher-tier plans — a copper ceiling that no plan upgrade can overcome. Both axes use a log scale spanning from 12 Mbps entry plans to 1 Gbps services.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)

Older technologies fail customers most often

The ACCC classifies a service as "underperforming" when it consistently delivers less than 75% of its advertised speed during busy hours. Fibre to the Node (FTTN) has the highest underperformance rate, reflecting physical limits of copper phone lines. FTTP and HFC rarely underperform because fibre delivers consistent speeds regardless of distance from the exchange.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)

Fibre reliably delivers; copper and satellite often fall short

Each bar shows what share of services deliver less than 75%, 75–90%, 90–110%, or more than 110% of their advertised plan speed. FTTP and HFC push almost all their services into the meets or exceeds categories. Satellite is the most unpredictable: 42% over-deliver (likely Starlink burst capacity) while 34% significantly under-deliver.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)

Australia's broadband speeds have soared since 2021

Comparing Ookla's 2021 crowd-sourced measurements with the ACCC's 2025 monitored panel reveals a striking shift. Average speeds have roughly quadrupled across most states, driven by uptake of higher-tier plans and the ongoing FTTP upgrade rollout. Note: the datasets measure different things — Ookla captures all Speedtest users, while the ACCC panel monitors specific broadband subscribers. Both confirm the strong upward trend.

Sources: Ookla Speedtest Open Data 2021 Q3 (CC BY 4.0)  ·  ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)
Part 4

A Deeper Look at the Data

The fibre–copper divide is visible at a glance

Each cell shows the average download speed for a geography–technology combination. Darker blue = faster. FTTP stays dark across all geographies; Satellite and FTTN are consistently pale. Rural areas gain almost nothing by upgrading from FTTN to Fixed Wireless — only full fibre moves the needle.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)

Nearly half the panel is now on fibre

Each square = 1% of the 1,787 monitored services. FTTP now holds 45 squares — the single largest block — thanks to the government's accelerated fibre upgrade. Yet legacy technologies (FTTN 18% + HFC 21%) still account for nearly 40% of all connections.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)

Higher plan tiers deliver more — but with far greater spread

Entry-level 25 Mbps plans are consistent: almost everyone gets close to 25 Mbps. But 500 Mbps and 1 Gbps plans show wide boxes — some households hit 900+ Mbps while others struggle below 200 Mbps. The spread at the top tiers reflects the technology mix: a 1 Gbps FTTP service delivers; a 1 Gbps FTTN service cannot.

Top-tier plans vary wildly 1 Gbps boxes span 200–950 Mbps because FTTN copper limits performance regardless of plan
Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)
Part 5

Trends & Geography

Fibre over-delivers; copper under-delivers

Each line traces one technology from its mean advertised speed to its mean actual busy-hour speed. Lines sloping upward mean the technology over-delivers on average; lines sloping downward mean it falls short. FTTP and HFC both rise — providers under-promise and over-deliver. FTTN and Satellite slope down, confirming systematic underperformance.

Source: ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025 (CC BY 2.5 AU)

State speed rankings reshuffled since 2021

The bump chart tracks each state's speed rank (1 = fastest) from Ookla 2021 to ACCC 2025. The ACT and VIC have held the top spots, but the middle pack has shuffled significantly as FTTP upgrades roll out unevenly. States that adopted fibre early climbed; those still relying on copper fell behind. Hover a line to see exact speeds.

Sources: Ookla Speedtest Open Data 2021 Q3  ·  ACCC MBA Report 28, 2025

Testing is concentrated in the south-east — and speed follows

Each circle is centred on a state's geographic centre. Circle size shows the number of Ookla Speedtest measurements from that state in Q3 2021; colour shows average download speed (darker = faster). NSW and VIC dominate testing volume, while the ACT — with far fewer tests — records the fastest average, reflecting its higher share of fibre connections and tech-savvy residents.

ACT leads Smallest circle, darkest blue: fewest tests but highest average speed at 61 Mbps
Source: Ookla Speedtest Open Data, 2021 Q3 (CC BY 4.0)
